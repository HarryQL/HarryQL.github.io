Philosophy
"The most powerful AI systems aren't the ones that ignore physics—they're the ones that deeply understand it and use machine learning to go beyond what equations alone can solve."

My path from Berkeley to MIT to Tesla has been defined by one consistent thread: making AI genuinely useful for scientific discovery and real world physical applications. At MIT, I built generative models that designed novel crystals and developed Bayesian optimization frameworks that reduced experimental costs by an order of magnitude. Now at Tesla, I'm applying those same principles to batteries—creating physics-informed neural networks that run in real-time firmware while respecting the electrochemistry that governs how cells actually behave.

I approach problems by first understanding the underlying science deeply, then identifying where ML/AI can provide leverage that pure simulation cannot. Whether it's using reinforcement learning for autonomous calibration, Bayesian optimization for experiment design, or neural network surrogates for real-time inference, the goal is always the same: build systems that are scientifically grounded, computationally tractable, and deployable in the real world.

How I work
01
Physics First, Learning Second

Every ML model I build starts with some level of physical understanding. Understanding governing equations, conservation laws, and system constraints isn't just good practice—it's what separates ML models that generalize from those that fail in production.

02
Close the Loop

The most impactful research connects seamlessly to action. I design systems for autonomous operation—from Bayesian optimization that runs experiments to physics-informed networks deployed in firmware control loops.

03
Interpretability Enables Trust

Black-box predictions aren't enough when safety matters. I prioritize interpretable models and hybrid approaches that let domain experts understand and validate what the AI is learning.

04
Open Science Compounds

From Hybrid-MPET battery simulation software, Bayesian Optimization framework, to the Materials Project Raman database, I believe open-source tools multiply impact. The best way to advance a field is to give others the infrastructure to build on your work.
